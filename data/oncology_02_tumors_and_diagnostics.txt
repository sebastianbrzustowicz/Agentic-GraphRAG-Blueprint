TUMORS — CLASSIFICATION, STAGING AND DIAGNOSTICS

DEFINITION AND GENERAL CLASSIFICATION
Tumors (Latin: tumores) are focal enlargements of tissues. They are divided into neoplastic tumors and non-neoplastic lesions mimicking tumors: cysts (fluid-filled; e.g. renal, ovarian, hepatic cysts, including hydatid cysts), abscesses, hematomas, enlarged lymph nodes, inflammatory lesions (pseudotumors), hyperplasia (physiological proliferation) and hamartomas (congenital developmental disorders, e.g. hemangioma). Neoplastic tumors are classified histogenetically — according to the tissue of origin.

HISTOGENETIC CLASSIFICATION
1) Epithelial tumors: benign — adenoma, papilloma; malignant — carcinomas: squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, small cell carcinoma, transitional cell carcinoma (bladder). 2) Mesenchymal tumors (connective tissue): benign — lipoma, fibroma, leiomyoma (e.g. uterine fibroid), chondroma, hemangioma; malignant — sarcomas: liposarcoma, fibrosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, osteosarcoma, angiosarcoma. 3) Melanocytic tumors: benign nevus and malignant melanoma. 4) Tumors of the hematopoietic and lymphoid system: leukemias (acute and chronic: AML, ALL, CML, CLL), Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphomas, plasma cell myeloma. 5) Neuroendocrine tumors: carcinoid, GEP-NET, medullary thyroid carcinoma. 6) Germ cell tumors: seminoma, teratoma. 7) Central nervous system tumors: meningioma (benign), glioma, astrocytoma (malignant). Sarcomas are rarer than carcinomas (about 1% of adult malignancies), more common in children.

TNM STAGING SYSTEM
TNM is the international classification system (AJCC/UICC): T describes the primary tumor (T0 — no tumor, Tis — carcinoma in situ, T1–T4 — increasing size/invasion), N describes regional lymph node involvement (N0 — none, N1–N3 — extent), M describes distant metastasis (M0 — none, M1 — present). The combination of TNM yields the clinical stage (I–IV): stages I–II are localized disease, III — regionally advanced, IV — disseminated. Stage is the most important prognostic factor and determines treatment strategy (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, systemic therapy).

HISTOLOGICAL GRADING (G)
Grading assesses microscopic malignancy: G1 — well differentiated (closest to the tissue of origin), G2 — moderately differentiated, G3 — poorly differentiated, G4 — undifferentiated (anaplastic). The higher the grade, the faster the growth and the worse the prognosis. Additional parameters: number of mitoses (Ki-67 proliferation index), nuclear atypia, necrosis, vascular invasion.

DIAGNOSTICS
Tumor diagnostics include: 1) history and physical examination (palpable masses, enlarged nodes); 2) imaging: ultrasound, X-ray, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), PET-CT (fluorodeoxyglucose — metabolic activity), bone scintigraphy; 3) laboratory tests: tumor markers (PSA — prostate, CEA — colorectal, CA 19-9 — pancreas, CA 125 — ovary, AFP — liver, beta-hCG — germ cell tumors), blood count (leukemias); 4) biopsy: fine-needle aspiration (FNA), core needle biopsy under US/CT guidance, surgical biopsy (excision); 5) histopathology with immunohistochemistry (markers: cytokeratins, CD20, HER2, ER/PR, PD-L1) and molecular testing (NGS, EGFR/KRAS/BRAF mutations, BRCA1/2, MSI/dMMR, ALK/ROS1 fusions). Histological confirmation remains the gold standard of diagnosis.

METASTASIS
Metastases are distant tumor deposits; routes of spread: hematogenous (venous — most often liver, lungs, bones, brain), lymphatic (regional nodes), transcoelomic (peritoneal, pleural cavities) and implantation (during procedures). The "seed and soil" concept explains organotropism — e.g. breast cancer metastasizes to bone and brain, colorectal cancer to the liver. The presence of metastases (M1) means disseminated disease and, most often, systemic rather than radical surgical treatment.

PARANEOPLASTIC SYNDROMES
Tumors may produce symptoms unrelated to direct invasion: hypercalcemia (PTHrP — lung, kidney cancer), SIADH (small cell lung cancer — hyponatremia), Cushing syndrome (ectopic ACTH), carcinoid syndrome (flushing, diarrhea — serotonin), acanthosis nigricans, thrombosis (Trousseau syndrome). Paraneoplastic symptoms may precede the cancer diagnosis.

SELECTED ORGAN-SPECIFIC CANCERS
Lung cancer — leading cause of cancer death; subtypes: non-small cell (NSCLC — about 85%: adenocarcinoma, squamous) and small cell (SCLC); risk factors: smoking, radon, asbestos; diagnostics: chest CT, PET-CT, biopsy, molecular testing (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, PD-L1). Breast cancer — most common cancer in women; receptor subtypes: luminal A/B (ER/PR+), HER2+, triple-negative (TNBC); treatment: surgery, radiotherapy, hormonal therapy, trastuzumab, immunotherapy. Colorectal cancer — often develops from adenomas (polyps) via the adenoma-carcinoma sequence (APC, KRAS, TP53 mutations, MSI); colonoscopy enables prevention (polypectomy). Prostate cancer — most common in men; sensitive marker PSA; often indolent (active surveillance possible at low risk). Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) — on a background of cirrhosis (HBV/HCV, alcohol, MASH); marker AFP, contrast-enhanced CT/MRI. Pancreatic cancer — very poor prognosis (5-year survival about 10%), late symptoms (jaundice, pain), marker CA 19-9. Renal cell carcinoma, bladder cancer (transitional cell; linked to smoking and schistosomiasis), thyroid cancer (papillary — excellent prognosis), gastric cancer (linked to H. pylori), brain tumors (gliomas — astrocytomas, glioblastoma; benign meningiomas). Hematologic malignancies: acute myeloid leukemia (AML), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL — the most common childhood cancer), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), lymphomas (Hodgkin — disease of young adults, excellent prognosis), plasma cell myeloma (monoclonal protein, osteolytic lesions).

BIOMARKERS, MOLECULAR TESTING AND SCREENING
Modern oncology relies on biomarkers: prognostic (predict outcome independently of therapy), predictive (predict treatment response — e.g. HER2 → trastuzumab, EGFR → erlotinib/osimertinib, ALK → alectinib, BRAF V600E → vemurafenib, PD-L1 → immunotherapy, MSI-H/dMMR → pembrolizumab in solid tumors) and monitoring markers (CEA in colorectal cancer, CA 125 in ovarian cancer, PSA). Germline genetic testing (BRCA1/2 — breast and ovarian cancer, Lynch syndrome — MLH1/MSH2, FAP — APC, retinoblastoma — RB1) enables familial risk assessment and prevention in carriers. Next-generation sequencing (NGS — multigene panels, ctDNA liquid biopsy) is entering standard diagnostics. Screening detects cancer in asymptomatic people: cervical — cytology (Pap) every 3 years or HPV-DNA test every 5 years; breast — mammography every 2 years (ages 50–69); colorectal — colonoscopy every 10 years or FIT every 1–2 years; prostate — PSA (shared decision); lung — low-dose CT in smokers aged 50–80 with ≥30 pack-years. Screening effectiveness is proven for cervical (about 70% mortality reduction in organized programs), breast, colorectal and lung cancer (NLST: 20% mortality reduction).

STAGING WORK-UP
Clinical staging combines physical examination, imaging (chest CT, abdominal US/CT, bone scintigraphy, PET-CT) and, in selected tumors, surgical staging (sentinel lymph node biopsy — SLNB — in breast cancer and melanoma; mediastinoscopy in lung cancer). Pathological staging (pTNM) obtained after surgery is more precise than clinical staging and may change treatment decisions (e.g. indication for adjuvant chemotherapy).
