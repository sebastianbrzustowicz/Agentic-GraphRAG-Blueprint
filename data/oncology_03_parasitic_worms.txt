PARASITIC WORMS (HELMINTHS) — GROUPS, LIFE CYCLES, DISEASES AND TREATMENT

CLASSIFICATION
Parasitic worms of humans and animals belong to three phyla: nematodes (Nematoda — roundworms), tapeworms (Cestoda — segmented flatworms) and flukes (Trematoda — unsegmented, leaf-shaped flatworms). Helminths are the most common parasites of humans: according to WHO, about 1.5 billion people — about 24% of the world's population — are infected with soil-transmitted helminths (STH); additionally, about 600 million people are infected with the intestinal threadworm Strongyloides stercoralis.

NEMATODES (STH AND OTHERS)
The main STH of humans: the giant roundworm (Ascaris lumbricoides) — the largest intestinal nematode (females up to 35 cm), the whipworm (Trichuris trichiura) and hookworms (Ancylostoma duodenale and Necator americanus). Life cycle: adult worms live in the intestine and produce thousands of eggs per day; eggs pass in feces into the soil, where they mature for about 3 weeks; infection occurs by ingesting eggs (unwashed vegetables, contaminated water, dirty hands in children) or — for hookworms — by larvae actively penetrating the skin (walking barefoot). S. stercoralis is exceptional: it can reproduce within the human host (auto-inoculation) and in immunocompromised individuals causes the hyperinfection syndrome with high mortality; it is not susceptible to albendazole or mebendazole and requires ivermectin. Other nematodes: human pinworm (Enterobius vermicularis — the most common in Europe, oral-anal transmission), trichinellosis (Trichinella spiralis — raw meat), filariae (Wuchereria bancrofti — elephantiasis), Onchocerca volvulus (river blindness), Dracunculus medinensis (contaminated water), visceral and cutaneous larva migrans (Toxocara, Ancylostoma braziliense). Symptoms of nematodiases: abdominal pain, diarrhea, malnutrition, iron-deficiency anemia (hookworms — chronic blood loss, especially dangerous in women of reproductive age and children), impaired growth and cognitive development in children, eosinophilia; massive A. lumbricoides infection can cause intestinal obstruction requiring surgery.

TAPEWORMS (CESTODA)
Tapeworms have a life cycle involving an intermediate host: taeniasis — pork tapeworm (Taenia solium) and beef tapeworm (T. saginata) — infection via raw pork (cysticerci in muscles) or beef; dangerous is cysticercosis — ingestion of T. solium eggs (fecal-oral) leads to larvae (cysticerci) in tissues, including neurocysticercosis in the brain — a frequent cause of epilepsy in developing countries; lesions on CT/MRI mimic brain tumors. Hydatid disease: Echinococcus granulosus — hydatid cysts in the liver and lungs, detected as focal "tumor-like" lesions; E. multilocularis — alveolar echinococcosis, a parasite invading like a malignancy, called "parasitic cancer" (high mortality; treatment with albendazole + surgery). The broad fish tapeworm (Diphyllobothrium latum) — infection via raw fish, causes vitamin B12 deficiency (megaloblastic anemia).

FLUKES (TREMATODA)
Schistosomes (Schistosoma mansoni, S. haematobium, S. japonicum) — schistosomiasis (bilharzia): infection via cercariae in fresh water that penetrate the skin; the chronic form leads to liver fibrosis and portal hypertension; S. haematobium is classified by IARC as carcinogenic to humans (group 1) — associated with squamous cell carcinoma of the urinary bladder. Liver flukes: Fasciola hepatica (infection via watercress), Opisthorchis viverrini and Clonorchis sinensis (raw fish) — chronic infestation is an IARC group 1 carcinogen for bile duct cancer (cholangiocarcinoma). The lung fluke (Paragonimus westermani) — raw crabs, nodular lung lesions; Paragonimus falls within the spectrum of fenbendazole.

TREATMENT AND CONTROL
WHO recommends periodic mass administration of anthelmintic drugs (preventive chemotherapy, MDA) to at-risk groups (preschool and school-age children, women of reproductive age) without individual diagnosis: albendazole 400 mg or mebendazole 500 mg — effective, inexpensive, with few adverse effects. Praziquantel is used for tapeworm and fluke infections (including schistosomiasis), ivermectin for strongyloidiasis and filariasis (generic, prequalified since 2021), nitazoxanide for giardiasis. In 2021 over 500 million children were treated (62% of the at-risk population); between 2010 and 2019 the number of DALYs lost to STH fell by more than 50%. Control also includes health education, access to water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH), wearing footwear and proper heat treatment of meat and fish (freezing, cooking). The WHO 2030 strategy (NTD roadmap) targets elimination of STH morbidity in children and strongyloidiasis control programs.

LABORATORY AND IMAGING DIAGNOSTICS
Parasitological diagnosis is the cornerstone: 1) stool examination for eggs and larvae (Kato-Katz method — quantitative standard for STH; flotation methods: Willis, Fülleborn, Decancq; for S. stercoralis — Baermann method and stool culture), 2) urine examination (S. haematobium eggs — filtration), 3) pinworm test (cellophane tape from the perianal area in the morning — Enterobius), 4) serology: ELISA, Western blot (anti-Echinococcus, anti-Schistosoma, anti-Trichinella, anti-Toxocara), 5) blood tests: eosinophilia (a clue, non-specific), immunoglobulin E, 6) imaging: ultrasound (hydatid cysts, liver fibrosis in schistosomiasis), CT/MRI (neurocysticercosis), X-ray (paragonimiasis). Eggs appear in stool only after a prepatent period of about 2–3 months, and light infections may show intermittent egg excretion.

WHO NUMBERS AND RISK GROUPS
About 260 million preschool children, 654 million school-age children, 108 million adolescent girls and 138.8 million pregnant and lactating women live in areas of intense STH transmission and require treatment and preventive interventions. In 2021 over 500 million children in endemic countries were treated with anthelmintics (62% of at-risk children), and more than 99 million women of reproductive age received albendazole through lymphatic filariasis elimination programs. Groups at particular risk of severe disease: immunocompromised patients (S. stercoralis — hyperinfection and dissemination syndrome, frequently fatal), malnourished children, pregnant women with anemia.

TOXOCARIASIS AND LARVA MIGRANS SYNDROMES
Toxocariasis (infection with Toxocara canis/cati larvae — dog and cat roundworms) presents as visceral larva migrans (VLM — fever, hepatosplenomegaly, eosinophilia, pulmonary lesions) or ocular larva migrans (OLM — retinal granuloma mimicking retinoblastoma in children — an important differential diagnosis with an ocular tumor!) and neurotoxocariasis (CNS involvement). Sources: eggs shed in animal feces, present in sandpits and on vegetables; prevention: deworming pets, hand and vegetable hygiene. Cutaneous larva migrans is caused by animal hookworm larvae (Ancylostoma braziliense, A. caninum) — serpiginous skin lesions; treatment: ivermectin, albendazole.

HELMINTHS, THE IMMUNE SYSTEM AND CANCER
Helminths induce a Th2 response and modulate the immune system (the so-called hygiene hypothesis — possible influence on allergic diseases). Chronic parasitic infections, especially S. haematobium, Opisthorchis and Clonorchis, increase the risk of selected cancers (bladder cancer, bile duct cancer) through chronic inflammation and immunomodulation. Conversely, many helminthiases present with eosinophilia that must be differentiated from neoplastic and myeloproliferative disorders.
