FENBENDAZOLE — VETERINARY ANTHELMINTIC FROM THE BENZIMIDAZOLE GROUP

CHEMISTRY AND REGULATORY STATUS
Fenbendazole is a synthetic antiparasitic drug from the benzimidazole group (a benzimidazole carbamate derivative). Molecular formula C15H13N3O2S, molar mass 299.35 g/mol, IUPAC name: methyl N-(6-phenylsulfanyl-1H-benzoimidazol-2-yl)carbamate; CAS number 43210-67-9. Veterinary ATC code QP52AC13 (human counterpart P02CA06 — assigned, but no human approval exists). Fenbendazole has NOT been tested or approved for use in humans by the FDA or EMA; no controlled clinical trials in humans exist. Related drugs of the same group approved for human use are albendazole and mebendazole; fenbendazole is also an active metabolite of the prodrug febantel, and is itself metabolized to oxfendazole (an anthelmintic also used in humans).

MECHANISM OF ACTION
Fenbendazole binds to the protein tubulin (β-tubulin subunit), the building block of microtubules, blocking their polymerization and function. In the parasite this leads to cytoskeleton breakdown, inhibition of intracellular transport and impaired uptake of glucose and other nutrients, resulting in the worm's death. It acts on adult and larval stages of many nematodes, some tapeworms (genus Taenia) and flukes (Paragonimus). Selectivity results from a higher affinity of the drug for parasite tubulin than for mammalian tubulin, which is the basis of its relative safety.

SPECTRUM AND VETERINARY USES
Fenbendazole is a broad-spectrum drug used in livestock and companion animals: dogs and cats — roundworms (Toxocara, Toxascaris), hookworms, whipworms, tapeworms of the genus Taenia (ineffective against Dipylidium caninum), Aelurostrongylus; horses — strongyles, pinworms, ascarids (paste form); cattle, sheep and goats — gastrointestinal nematodes, lungworms, flukes (partially); poultry, fish, rabbits, reptiles, and even seals, as well as planaria and hydra in aquarium tanks. It is administered as granules, paste, oral suspension or boluses; in cattle and sheep via feed, drenching or slow-release bolus. It is not effective against all tapeworms (Dipylidium) and mixed infections may require repeated dosing.

VETERINARY DOSING (EXAMPLES)
Standard fenbendazole doses: dogs and cats — 50 mg/kg body weight orally once daily for 3 consecutive days (5 days for giardiasis); horses — 7.5 mg/kg (oral paste; 10 mg/kg for 5 days in strongyloidosis); cattle and sheep — 7.5 mg/kg (oral drench) or 10 mg/kg in feed; poultry — 20–30 mg/kg. Its advantages are a high therapeutic index and wide dosing window; its drawback is growing nematode resistance (especially equine strongyles and Haemonchus contortus in sheep), requiring rotation of drug classes and targeted selective treatment (FAMACHA, FECRT).

PHARMACOKINETICS AND METABOLISM
Fenbendazole is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract in most species (low oral bioavailability — a significant limitation for systemic use). In the liver it is metabolized to oxfendazole (sulfoxide — also anthelmintically active), which is partially reduced back to fenbendazole in the liver and rumen; the main excreted metabolite is fenbendazole sulfone. Its effect is prolonged by enterohepatic circulation.

COMPARISON WITH RELATED BENZIMIDAZOLES
Mebendazole (human drug; spectrum of nematodes and tapeworms; 100–500 mg/day) and albendazole (400 mg; broader spectrum, including Echinococcus and flukes; used in long cycles for hydatid disease) have better oral bioavailability than fenbendazole. Oxfendazole (a fenbendazole metabolite) is approved for humans in selected countries. A common feature of benzimidazoles is low solubility and poor absorption, which — for systemic indications (e.g. cancer) — requires high doses, modified formulations (nanoparticles, cyclodextrin complexes) or alternative routes. Febantel is a prodrug converted to fenbendazole and oxfendazole — used in combination products for dogs and cats.

TOXICITY AND SAFETY
Acute toxicity is low: the oral LD50 in laboratory animals exceeds 10 g/kg body weight. When used as directed the drug is well tolerated; possible mild gastrointestinal upset (nausea, diarrhea). Prolonged administration to dogs at doses above the label recommendation has been associated with reported cases of bone marrow hypoplasia and pancytopenia — a mechanism related to microtubule inhibition in dividing cells; blood count monitoring is required. In humans taking fenbendazole off-label, two cases of serious liver injury have been reported (elevated transaminases, cholestasis), which resolved after drug discontinuation; hypoglycemia has also been described. There are no safety data for pregnant and lactating women; in animals fenbendazole has been used with caution in pregnancy — it is not recommended in pregnant animals without veterinary advice.

DRUG INTERACTIONS
Significant interactions occur with salicylanilides (dibromsalan, niclosamide) — concurrent administration to cattle and sheep caused abortions in cattle and deaths in sheep; abortions in ruminants have been reported with concurrent antitrematode agents. In humans no interactions are established because the drug is not approved.

SIGNIFICANCE IN THE MEDIA AND RESEARCH
Fenbendazole gained notoriety on social media after the popularization of the so-called Joe Tippens protocol (fenbendazole + cannabidiol + curcumin) as an alleged cancer cure. Credible data are limited to laboratory in vitro studies, animal models and isolated case reports; experts (including a review in Anticancer Research 2024, Nguyen et al.) emphasize that anticancer efficacy in humans remains unproven, and poor oral bioavailability is the main barrier. Use without medical supervision carries the risk of hepatotoxicity, pancytopenia and delay of effective oncological treatment.

RESEARCH DIRECTIONS IN ONCOLOGY
Preclinical work on fenbendazole as an anticancer agent focuses on: 1) combination strategies — studies suggest additive or synergistic effects with radiotherapy and selected cytostatics (e.g. taxanes, platinum compounds) in cell lines and animal models, which could allow lower doses of cytotoxics; 2) radiosensitization — microtubule-dependent mitotic arrest may enhance radiation-induced cell death; 3) targeting cancer stem cells (CSC) and the Warburg effect (GLUT1 inhibition) as a mechanism complementary to classical chemotherapy; 4) formulation science — improving oral bioavailability through nanoparticles, lipid-based formulations, cyclodextrin complexes or prodrugs, and exploring oxfendazole as a more bioavailable alternative. None of these lines of research has reached clinical trials in humans; all data remain preclinical. The evidence base is insufficient to recommend fenbendazole for any oncological indication, and its use should not delay or replace proven therapy.
