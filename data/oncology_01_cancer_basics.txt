CANCER — FUNDAMENTALS OF ONCOLOGY, EPIDEMIOLOGY AND CARCINOGENESIS

DEFINITION AND NATURE OF THE DISEASE
Cancer (neoplasia, from Latin tumor/neoplasma) is an abnormal, clonal proliferation of cells that have lost control over growth and differentiation. Clonality means that all cells of a tumor derive from a single mutated progenitor cell. Neoplasms are broadly divided into benign and malignant. A benign tumor grows slowly and expansively, is usually well demarcated, often encapsulated, does not invade surrounding tissues and does not metastasize; after complete surgical removal it does not recur. A malignant tumor (carcinoma in humans; any malignant growth in veterinary medicine) invades and destroys adjacent tissues, metastasizes via blood and lymphatic vessels, and exhibits anaplasia — loss of differentiation and cellular polymorphism. Tumors of the hematopoietic system (leukemias, lymphomas) are by definition disseminated, so they do not form a primary mass in the classical sense.

EPIDEMIOLOGY (GLOBOCAN 2022)
According to IARC/WHO data (GLOBOCAN 2022), about 20.0 million new cancer cases were diagnosed worldwide in 2022 and 9.7 million deaths were recorded. It is estimated that 1 in 5 people will develop cancer during their lifetime; the risk of death affects 1 in 9 men and 1 in 12 women. The most frequently diagnosed cancers worldwide: lung cancer (about 2.5 million new cases), breast cancer (2.3 million), colorectal cancer (1.9 million), prostate cancer (1.5 million) and stomach cancer (about 0.97 million). The leading causes of cancer death: lung cancer (about 1.8 million), colorectal cancer (about 0.9 million), liver cancer (about 0.76 million), breast cancer (about 0.67 million) and stomach cancer (about 0.66 million). In Poland about 170–180 thousand new cases are diagnosed each year; the "big five" are lung cancer, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, prostate cancer and bladder cancer. Incidence increases with age — two-thirds of cases affect people over 65.

CARCINOGENESIS — A MULTI-STEP PROCESS
The development of cancer requires the accumulation of mutations in genes that regulate the cell cycle: oncogenes (e.g. RAS, MYC, EGFR, HER2 — activating mutations), tumor suppressor genes (e.g. TP53, RB1, APC, BRCA1/2 — loss of function) and DNA repair genes (e.g. MLH1, MSH2 — microsatellite instability). A typical carcinoma develops over years through promotion and progression, involving clones of increasing aggressiveness. Carcinogens are divided into chemical (benzene, aflatoxins, nitrosamines, tobacco smoke — over 60 carcinogenic substances), physical (UV radiation — skin cancer, melanoma; ionizing radiation — leukemias, thyroid cancer), biological (oncogenic viruses: HPV — cervical and oral cancer; HBV/HCV — hepatocellular carcinoma; EBV — Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma; HHV-8 — Kaposi sarcoma; the bacterium Helicobacter pylori — gastric cancer; parasites — Schistosoma haematobium, liver flukes) and chronic inflammation (inflammatory bowel disease, reflux). It is estimated that about 30% of cancer deaths can be attributed to modifiable factors: tobacco smoking accounts for about 15% of cancer deaths worldwide, and infections for about 13–15% of cases globally.

HALLMARKS OF CANCER
Hanahan and Weinberg described the universal traits of cancer cells: 1) self-sufficiency in growth signals, 2) insensitivity to growth-inhibitory signals, 3) evasion of apoptosis, 4) replicative immortality (telomerase activation), 5) induction of angiogenesis, 6) capacity for invasion and metastasis, 7) deregulation of cellular metabolism (the Warburg effect — preference for aerobic glycolysis), 8) evasion of the immune response, 9) genomic instability and mutagenesis, 10) tumor-promoting inflammation. These hallmarks underpin modern targeted therapies and immunotherapy (PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 checkpoint inhibitors).

RED FLAG SYMPTOMS AND EARLY DETECTION
Symptoms requiring urgent oncological work-up include: unintentional weight loss (loss of >5% of body weight within 6 months), night sweats and low-grade fever, hemoptysis, gastrointestinal bleeding (melena, blood in stool), hematuria, persistent cough or hoarseness, a palpable mass, change in bowel habits, dysphagia, bone pain, new or changing skin lesions, enlarged lymph nodes. The "three-week rule" (any symptom lasting more than 3 weeks warrants assessment) applies, among others, to colorectal cancer. Early detection is critical because prognosis deteriorates dramatically in advanced stages: 5-year survival in stage I breast cancer is about 99%, in stage IV about 30%; in stage I lung cancer 60–70%, in stage IV only a few percent.

CANCER TREATMENT
Treatment depends on tumor type, stage, biology and patient status: 1) surgery — the mainstay for early-stage solid tumors (R0 resection), organ-sparing surgery (e.g. breast-conserving therapy, TME in rectal cancer), metastasectomy (liver); 2) radiotherapy — alone (laryngeal, prostate cancer) or adjuvant; techniques: IMRT, SBRT, brachytherapy, radiosurgery (gamma knife); 3) systemic therapy: chemotherapy (platinum agents, taxanes, anthracyclines, 5-FU), hormonal therapy (ER/PR blockade, antiandrogens), targeted therapies (kinase inhibitors: EGFR, ALK, BRAF; antibodies: trastuzumab — HER2, rituximab — CD20), immunotherapy (PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors: pembrolizumab, nivolumab; CAR-T), 4) supportive and palliative care (WHO analgesic ladder, management of complications, hospice care). Personalized medicine — selecting therapy based on the tumor's molecular profile (genomics, PD-L1, TMB) — is increasingly standard.

PROGNOSIS AND FOLLOW-UP
After curative treatment, patients require oncological surveillance (follow-up examinations, imaging, markers) to detect recurrence early. The 5-year relative survival for all cancers combined in Poland is about 50–55%, lower than the European average (about 60%), mainly due to late diagnosis; the highest survival is seen in thyroid (>95%) and testicular cancer (>95%) and early melanoma, the lowest in pancreatic (about 8–10%), liver and lung cancer. Globally, thanks to therapeutic progress, age-standardized mortality has been declining since the 1990s despite rising incidence.

PREVENTION
Primary prevention eliminates or reduces exposure to risk factors: smoking cessation (the single most effective intervention — lung cancer risk falls by about 50% after 10 years of abstinence), limiting alcohol, maintaining a healthy body weight and physical activity (reduces the risk of breast, colorectal and endometrial cancer), a healthy diet (high fiber, limited processed meat), sun protection (UV index awareness, sunscreen, avoiding tanning beds) and occupational protection. Vaccination: the HPV vaccine (prevents cervical, vulvar, vaginal, anal and oropharyngeal cancer — efficacy approaching 100% against persistent infection with vaccine-type strains) and the HBV vaccine (prevents hepatitis B and thereby most hepatocellular carcinoma cases). It is estimated that about 40% of cancers are preventable through lifestyle measures and infection control.
