FENBENDAZOLE AND CANCER — EVIDENCE, MECHANISMS, CONTROVERSIES AND DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

THE CONCEPT OF REPURPOSING ANTIPARASITIC DRUGS
Fenbendazole is being investigated as a candidate for repurposing in oncology. The rationale: benzimidazoles act on microtubules — the target of classical cytotoxics (taxanes, vinca alkaloids) — while being cheap, well characterized and used for decades in veterinary medicine. Related drugs — mebendazole, albendazole, flubendazole — and other antiparasitic agents (niclosamide, ivermectin, chloroquine) are also the subject of preclinical studies as potential anticancer agents. It must be emphasized: fenbendazole is not approved for cancer treatment in humans, no controlled clinical trials exist, and available data come from in vitro studies, animal models and isolated off-label case reports.

POSTULATED ANTICANCER MECHANISMS
1) Inhibition of microtubule polymerization → cell cycle arrest in G2/M phase and mitotic death of cancer cells. 2) Disruption of glucose metabolism: benzimidazoles may inhibit the glucose transporter GLUT1 and glycolysis, targeting the Warburg effect — the preferential use of aerobic glycolysis by cancer cells; this is a mechanism distinct from classical cytotoxics. 3) Induction of apoptosis: caspase activation, altered Bcl-2/Bax ratios, involvement of the p53 pathway. 4) Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. 5) Inhibition of angiogenesis (suggested for mebendazole). In vitro, fenbendazole showed cytotoxicity against cell lines: non-small cell lung cancer (A549), mouse breast cancer (EMT6, 4T1), lymphoma, colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer and glioma; in mouse xenograft models, tumor growth inhibition was observed. A review in Anticancer Research (2024, Nguyen et al.) concludes: preclinical data are promising but no clinical trials in humans exist; poor oral bioavailability is the main barrier.

REVIEW OF PRECLINICAL AND ANECDOTAL DATA
Fenbendazole demonstrated in vitro cytotoxic activity against the tumor cell lines listed above; mechanisms included G2/M arrest, apoptosis induction (caspases, Bcl-2/Bax), inhibition of the GLUT1 glucose transporter (metabolism — Warburg effect) and oxidative stress. In animal models (mice with xenografts) a reduction of tumor volume was observed, and some studies suggested an effect on cancer stem cells (CSC). Analogous results were obtained for mebendazole (including studies on gliomas — growth inhibition in animal models) and flubendazole. The systematic review in Anticancer Research (2024) concludes: oral fenbendazole has potential as a candidate for adjunctive therapy, but there are no phase I–III clinical trials in humans whatsoever, and poor oral bioavailability, unknown human pharmacokinetics (dose, schedule, long-term safety) remain unresolved.

THE TIPPENS PROTOCOL AND THE SOCIAL MEDIA PHENOMENON
The "Joe Tippens protocol" (popularized in 2019): 222 mg of fenbendazole (1 g of 22.2% granules) orally for 3 days, then a 4-day break, combined with CBD oil and curcumin (piperine), as an alleged adjunctive therapy for advanced cancers. The story is based on a single undocumented case report and has never been the subject of any study. Social media (YouTube, forums) multiply "testimonials of cure" in which patients concurrently used chemotherapy, radiotherapy or surgery — making it impossible to attribute the effect to fenbendazole. The FDA has warned against purchasing veterinary fenbendazole products for human use (no quality control, unknown contaminants, inappropriate dosing), and oncology communities point to real dangers: hepatotoxicity, bone marrow suppression, hypoglycemia and delay of effective treatment. Fenbendazole appears on lists of unproven cancer therapies.

WHY DOSES MATTER
Effective doses in laboratory animals are 50–100 mg/kg/day, which for a 70 kg human would mean 3.5–7 g/day — whereas the "protocol" recommends 222 mg. Given fenbendazole's poor oral bioavailability (a few to a dozen or so percent in most species), reaching therapeutic concentrations in tumor tissue is unlikely; hence work on formulations with improved bioavailability (nanoparticles, prodrugs, intravenous administration in animals) and on metabolites (oxfendazole). These pharmacokinetic limitations are the main argument against simply transferring veterinary results to humans.

CONTROVERSY AND DISINFORMATION
On social media fenbendazole is often presented as a "miracle cancer cure" — usually based on emotional patient stories of those who concurrently received chemotherapy, radiotherapy or surgery, which precludes attributing efficacy to fenbendazole. WHO, FDA and EMA do not recommend its use in humans; it is listed among unproven cancer therapies. Risks of self-medication: hepatotoxicity (documented liver injury in humans), pancytopenia (with long-term use in animals), hypoglycemia, drug interactions and — most dangerous — delay in implementing effective, evidence-based oncological treatment. Any use should occur exclusively under medical supervision, ideally within clinical trials.

PARASITES AND CANCER — TWO SIDES OF THE RELATIONSHIP
Parasites can be both causes and mimickers of cancer. As causes: IARC classifies as carcinogenic to humans (group 1) the blood fluke Schistosoma haematobium (squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder), the liver flukes Opisthorchis viverrini and Clonorchis sinensis (cholangiocarcinoma — bile duct cancer) and (group 2B) Schistosoma japonicum; the mechanism is chronic inflammation, immunomodulation and epithelial damage. As mimickers: parasitic lesions on imaging resemble tumors — Echinococcus granulosus hydatid cysts in the liver (focal lesions differentiated from hepatocellular carcinoma and cysts), alveolar echinococcosis E. multilocularis (an infiltrating "parasitic cancer" of the liver), neurocysticercosis (brain lesions mimicking tumors, a frequent cause of epilepsy), paragonimiasis (nodular lung lesions differentiated from lung cancer), massive ascariasis (intestinal obstruction), schistosomiasis (bladder lesions, hematuria). Differential diagnosis requires imaging (CT, MRI, US), serology (anti-Echinococcus, anti-Schistosoma antibodies), parasitological examination (eggs in stool/urine), histopathology and tumor markers.

CLINICAL RELEVANCE FOR ONCOLOGY
These issues show that: 1) in patients with an "unusual-looking tumor", a parasitic etiology should be considered (especially with an epidemiological history: travel, consumption of raw fish/meat, contact with animals); 2) chronic helminthiases are a modifiable risk factor for cancer (their treatment may reduce risk); 3) antiparasitic drugs, including fenbendazole, remain at the laboratory stage as potential adjunctive therapies — without clinical evidence they should not replace standard oncological treatment (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapies).

THE ROLE OF THE PHYSICIAN AND ETHICS
Cancer patients seeking unconventional therapies should receive honest information: fenbendazole is not an approved drug, there is no clinical evidence of efficacy, and off-label use carries documented toxicity risk. Rather than dismissing the topic, the physician should discuss the research status (preclinical), offer participation in clinical trials and emphasize the importance of standard treatment. Concurrent use of unverified substances may alter the pharmacokinetics of cytotoxics (CYP450 interactions, glucuronidation) and confound assessment of treatment response.

SUMMARY
Fenbendazole: a proven veterinary drug against nematodes, tapeworms and some flukes; in oncology — only preclinical research and anecdotal reports; no FDA/EMA approval; known risk of hepatotoxicity. Cancer: a multi-step clonal disease with well-characterized biology (hallmarks of cancer). Worms: the most common parasitic infections in the world (1.5 billion people), some of which are recognized carcinogens. These three areas converge in clinical practice in differential diagnosis and in the search for new therapies.
